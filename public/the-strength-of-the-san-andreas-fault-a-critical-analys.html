1. Introduction

The strength of the San Andreas fault has been debated for more than 35 years. It was initiated by heat flow measurements that failed to show a narrow heat flow anomaly centered on the fault that would be expected from a conductive model of frictionally generated heat [Brune, et al., 1969]. This suggested that the shear stress on the fault is very small, no greater that the stress drops in earthquakes, ~ 10 MPa, as opposed to the values of ~ 100MPa, expected from laboratory values of rock friction.

This debate, sometimes called the San Andreas heat flow paradox, but which I refer to as the weak SAF hypothesis, spawned two major research programs. One was to try to find a mechanism or mechanisms by which the fault could be so weak. The other was to conduct stress measurements in an attempt to verify the hypothesis. The net result is that the issue has yet to be clearly resolved.

The three elements of the puzzle, heat flow measurements, stress measurements, and friction measurement and theory, are in conflict. Here I critically review this debate, and make an attempt at providing a solution to the conflicts.
